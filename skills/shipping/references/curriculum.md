# Master curriculum — raw material, adapt freely

Select, merge, reorder, cut to fit the learner. Each level ends with a boss fight that is a real gate. Every exercise runs on the learner's own real changes.

**Non-code work uses the same curriculum.** Read "change" as any deliverable, "diff" as the before/after or tracked changes, "reviewer" as the editor, client, or stakeholder, and "merged" as accepted. Every lesson below holds; only the artifacts change.

## LEVEL 1 — The shipping gap *(short — 2–3 sessions — but never skip it; everything else builds on this mental model)*

- **1.1 Generated is not done.** The mental model: an agent finishing is an event that happened to *you*; shipping is a claim you make to *someone else*. Between them sits work nobody assigned you. Why "it runs" and "the tests pass" are the beginning of a review, not the end of one. Exercise: take something they shipped or nearly shipped, and list everything that was true of it beyond "it worked."
- **1.2 Generating is cheap, reviewing is not.** The economics that drive every norm in this course: a change costs its author minutes and its reviewer hours, so the cost of sloppy output lands on somebody who did not choose it. Maintainers are closing contributions with two words because the volume outran the attention. Exercise: estimate the review cost of their last change in reviewer-minutes — lines to read, decisions to verify, context to reconstruct — and compare it to what the change cost them to produce.
- **1.3 Shipping triage.** The filter before handing anything over: *Who reviews this? How much will it cost them? What happens if it's wrong and how fast can it be undone?* Small, reversible, self-reviewed work needs little ceremony. Large, irreversible, or other-people's-repo work needs all of it. Exercise: triage 6 of their recent or planned changes into tiers; this becomes their personal shipping triage card.
- **1.4 The rejection taxonomy.** What actually gets work bounced, in rough order of frequency: too many changes at once; scope creep the reviewer didn't ask for; unexplained "while I was in there" edits; formatting churn burying the real change; deleted or weakened tests; claims that don't match the diff; no explanation of *why*. Note that correctness is rarely the top reason. Exercise: they sort 8 short change descriptions into the taxonomy and predict which get rejected.
- **1.5 The spot-the-problem game (recurring ritual).** Present a plausible agent-generated change with 2–4 planted shipping problems drawn from the taxonomy — an unrequested refactor, a quietly widened permission, a test that asserts nothing, a claim in the description the diff doesn't support. They hunt them. **Always reveal the complete answer key before the exercise closes.** This game returns every few sessions with subtler material.
- **Boss fight (Level 1):** hand them a sprawling real change (theirs, or one you construct in their stack) and have them predict every reason a reviewer would push back — before any fix. Pass = Level 2.

## LEVEL 2 — Scoping so it lands

- **2.1 One change, one purpose.** The single highest-leverage habit: a change that does one thing can be reviewed in one pass. A change that does three requires the reviewer to hold three models at once and they will simply ask you to split it. Exercise: take a multi-purpose change of theirs and name every separate thing it does.
- **2.2 Splitting into a landable sequence.** How to turn one big change into an ordered series where each step is independently reviewable and independently correct — and how to order them so the risky part is last. Exercise: split the change from 2.1 into a real sequence and land the first piece.
- **2.3 Saying no to the agent's helpfulness.** Agents volunteer: renaming things, reformatting files, "improving" adjacent code, adding dependencies. Every unrequested edit is review cost you imposed on someone. How to constrain up front and how to strip after. Exercise: run a real task, then remove everything from the diff that wasn't asked for.
- **2.4 Writing a brief the agent can finish.** Scope stated as a boundary, not a wish: what to touch, what not to touch, what "done" looks like, what to do when it hits something unexpected instead of improvising. (Cross-reference `/ai-docent:prompt-craft` for the craft of the brief itself.) Exercise: write the brief, run it, and measure how much of the diff was in scope.
- **2.5 Stopping at done.** Recognizing the finish line and resisting one more improvement — including the agent's offer of one. Why "while we're here" is how a 20-line change becomes a 400-line one. Exercise: define done in advance for a real task, then stop there and note what they were tempted to add.
- **Boss fight (Level 2):** take a task that would naturally sprawl and land it as a change small enough that a reviewer could read it in five minutes, with nothing in the diff that wasn't required. Pass = Level 3.

## LEVEL 3 — Reviewing your own work *(you are the first reviewer, and often the only one)*

- **3.1 Read the diff before anyone else does.** The non-negotiable habit: every line, in diff form, before you hand it over. Not the agent's summary of the diff — the diff. How to read one when you didn't write it, and what to do when it's too long to read (that's a scoping signal, go back to Level 2). Exercise: review their own most recent change line by line and count what surprises them.
- **3.2 Claims of work are not work.** The agent says the tests pass; did they run? It says it checked; did it? Demand the actual output, re-run it yourself, verify the claim against the artifact. (This is the shipping face of the same discipline `/ai-docent:reliability` teaches for facts.) Exercise: catch a plausible "all green" claim by demanding the real output.
- **3.3 What the agent didn't mention.** The dangerous category is omission, not error: a deleted test, a changed default, a widened permission, a swapped dependency, an error path quietly swallowed. These rarely appear in the summary. Exercise: audit a real diff specifically for removals and defaults — what got smaller or looser?
- **3.4 Your self-review checklist.** Build their personal 60-second pre-handover ritual from the failures they actually keep hitting — not a generic list. It has to be short enough that they'll really run it. Exercise: draft it, run it on a real change, cut anything that didn't earn its place.
- **3.5 Reviewing at volume.** When there's more output than attention: sample the riskiest parts first, read every deletion, read every file you didn't expect to be touched, and know when the honest answer is "this is too big to review, split it." Exercise: a large change, reviewed under a strict time budget, defending what they chose to read.
- **3.6 The clean-context second opinion.** A fresh session with no memory of building it, given only the diff and asked to find problems — because the session that produced a change is biased toward defending it. Exercise: run a real change through a clean-room review and compare to their own.
- **Boss fight (Level 3):** you plant 3 shipping problems in a realistic change in their stack; their checklist and habits must catch all 3, and they must say which guardrail caught each. Full disclosure after. Pass = Level 4.

## LEVEL 4 — Landing it with other people *(the graduation level)*

- **4.1 The description that gets it merged.** What a reviewer needs before reading a single line: what this changes, why, what you deliberately left out, and how you verified it. Why "what I didn't do" prevents half of all review round-trips. Exercise: write descriptions for two real changes; test them on someone who lacks the context.
- **4.2 Someone else's house rules.** Reading CONTRIBUTING before writing a line, matching the project's conventions rather than your agent's defaults, checking whether the project has a stated policy on AI-assisted contributions, and starting small enough to earn trust. Exercise: pick a real project, read its contribution norms, and plan a first change that fits them.
- **4.3 Disclosure and honesty about how it was made.** Some projects require it, some forbid the practice, most are figuring it out. The durable rule: never imply more human authorship than there was, and never claim verification you didn't do. Exercise: draft their standing disclosure line and check it against two real projects' policies.
- **4.4 Taking feedback without regenerating everything.** The failure mode where one review comment triggers a fresh generation and the reviewer has to start over. Respond to what was asked, keep the diff stable, make follow-ups legible. Exercise: apply real feedback as a minimal follow-up rather than a rewrite.
- **4.5 When it bounces.** A rejection with no explanation is data, not a verdict. How to ask a maintainer a question worth answering, how to diagnose from the taxonomy in 1.4, and when the right move is to fork and move on. Exercise: take a real rejection (theirs or a public one) and reconstruct the likely reason.
- **4.6 Team norms.** For people shipping alongside others: agreeing what agent-assisted work looks like here — size limits, review expectations, what must be human-verified, who carries the cost. A norm nobody agreed to is a norm nobody follows. Exercise: draft a one-page team agreement they could actually propose.
- **4.7 Graduation — land it.** Take one real change from brief to merged: scoped small, self-reviewed against their own checklist, described so a stranger could review it, and landed with whoever reviews their work. Grade honestly — what was clean, what needed a round-trip, what would have bounced with a stricter reviewer. Then write a short "shipping diploma": strengths, the habit most likely to bite them, and the three rituals to keep forever. Graduation also produces a one-page **keepable cheat-sheet** — their triage card, their self-review checklist, their description template.

## WOVEN THROUGHOUT (every session, no exceptions)

- The mantra: *generating is cheap, reviewing is not; one change, one purpose; read the diff before anyone else does.*
- Every lesson runs on their real work — real diffs, real repos, real reviewers. Never a toy example.
- Smaller is the win condition. Celebrate a change that lands small more than one that lands big.
- Keep a running **Landed log** — every change that landed or bounced gets one line: what it was, how big, what happened, what you'd do differently. Bounces are worth more than merges; review it at retros.
- Blast radius before speed: know what breaks and how fast it can be undone before you hand anything over.
- Staying current: project norms and platform review tooling change — when something learned here stops matching what a project actually asks for, the project wins, and update your notes.
