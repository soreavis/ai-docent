# Master curriculum — raw material, adapt freely

Select, merge, reorder, cut to fit the learner. Each level ends with a boss fight that is a real gate.

## LEVEL 1 — Know your enemy *(short — 2–3 sessions — but never skip it; everything else builds on this mental model)*

- **1.1 Why AI makes things up.** Plain-language mental model: Claude predicts plausible text, it doesn't look facts up in a database. Fluency and confidence are not evidence. Key consequence: it fails *plausibly* — wrong answers look exactly like right ones. Exercise: they guess which of 5 statements an AI is most likely to get wrong and why; discuss what the answers have in common (obscure, specific, numeric, recent).
- **1.2 The fabrication field guide.** A taxonomy used all course, with one live demonstration of each where possible: (a) fabricated facts and details, (b) fake or mangled citations/links/quotes, (c) invented numbers and statistics, (d) confabulated APIs, functions, packages, and settings, (e) "pretend work" — an agent claiming it ran/tested/checked something it didn't, (f) sycophancy — telling them what they want to hear, (g) stale knowledge presented as current, (h) misread inputs — confidently wrong readings of charts, tables, images, or screenshots (vision isn't exact). Exercise: they sort 10 real-looking examples into the taxonomy.
- **1.3 Risk triage — when accuracy matters.** The two-question filter before any task: *Will someone act on this?* and *Is it hard to undo?* Brainstorming and drafts = low stakes, verify lightly. Decisions, money, health, grades, published things, running code = high stakes, full guardrails. Exercise: triage 8 tasks from their real life into verification tiers; this becomes their personal triage card.
- **1.4 The spot-the-fake game (recurring ritual).** Generate a short briefing on a topic they know, with 2–4 planted fabrications spanning the taxonomy. They hunt them. **Always reveal the complete answer key afterwards and never leave planted errors uncorrected.** This game returns every few sessions with harder material — fake citations, subtly wrong numbers, plausible invented details.
- **1.5 The human failure — automation bias.** The subtler risk isn't Claude lying; it's *them* getting comfortable and waving things through. Name the trap: using these tools harder and trusting them less can happen at once, and the gap between the two usually gets closed by waving things through anyway rather than by checking more. As AI gets things right most of the time, guard drops and verification quietly erodes — exactly when a rare confident error slips past. Countermeasure: tie verification effort to *stakes*, not to *how much you trust the tool today*, and keep the high-stakes rituals non-negotiable even 50 good answers in. Exercise: review their own last week of AI use — where did they stop checking, and what would being wrong there have cost?

## LEVEL 2 — Guardrails in Claude chats (claude.ai)

- **2.1 Honest prompting.** Patterns that change Claude's behavior: explicitly permitting "I don't know" ("if you're not sure, say so — I prefer honesty over completeness"), asking for confidence levels and what would change the answer, asking "what part of this answer are you least sure about?" after any important response. Exercise: same hard question asked plain vs. with honesty patterns; compare.
- **2.2 Grounding — the single biggest upgrade.** Give Claude the source material instead of relying on its memory: upload the document, then "answer ONLY from this document; quote the exact passage that supports each claim; if it's not in the document, say 'not in the document'". For a long document, make quote extraction its own step — pull the passages first, then answer from the passages — and put the document before the question, not after it. Exercise: real document from their high-stakes zone, grounded Q&A, and they check two quotes against the original themselves.
- **2.3 Web search done right.** When to force a search (anything recent, anything specific, anything they'll act on), demanding sources, and the golden rule: **a citation is a claim, not proof — click it.** How to spot fake/mismatched citations: source doesn't exist, exists but doesn't say that, says it but out of context. Exercise: research task in their high-stakes zone; they personally verify 3 cited claims at the source; score the results.
- **2.4 Numbers don't lie — unless they're predicted.** Never trust mental arithmetic, statistics, or data summaries at face value; make Claude actually compute (analysis/code execution) instead of estimating, and spot-check one number by hand or with a calculator. Exercise: give Claude a small real dataset, get analysis two ways (asked plainly vs. forced computation), find the discrepancies.
- **2.5 Beating sycophancy.** Claude tends to agree with the framing it's given. Countermeasures: neutral phrasing ("evaluate this idea" not "isn't this a great idea?"), asking for the strongest case AGAINST, "what would a skeptical expert say?", and red-teaming their own plans. Exercise: they pitch a real idea twice — leading vs. neutral — and compare the answers; then formally red-team the idea.
- **2.6 The second-opinion technique, briefed to refute.** Fresh chat, zero shared context, as an independent reviewer: paste the output (not the conversation) and ask it to fact-check, find errors, and challenge conclusions. Why context contamination matters — a chat that produced an answer is biased toward defending it. Then sharpen the brief: a reviewer asked to *review* tends to approve; one whose only job is to *refute* has to produce something or admit it couldn't. The refuter's rules: every refutation names the exact claim, says what is wrong with it, and says what evidence would settle it — "looks fine" and "seems off" both score zero, and "I could not refute it" is the honest null result, meaning *survived one attempt*, not *correct*. For high-stakes outputs run two or three refuters in separate chats and tally: one grounded refutation outweighs any number of nulls. A refutation is itself a claim — check it at the source before acting on it, because a refuter fabricates as readily as an author. Exercise: run a real output of theirs through a clean-room review, then through two blind refuters; tally the verdicts and verify one refutation at its source.
- **2.7 Your standing honesty config.** Turn the best patterns from 2.1–2.6 into permanent infrastructure: personal preferences / project custom instructions that encode honesty rules (admit uncertainty, cite sources, flag low-confidence claims, never invent quotes or links). Draft, test, and install **their Honesty Config v1** together. Exercise: same question in a bare chat vs. the configured project; document the difference.
- **2.8 Level 2 boss fight.** Produce a polished, realistic research brief in their high-stakes zone containing planted fabrications of at least 4 taxonomy types. They must find them using only this level's techniques, document HOW they caught each one, and state which guardrail would have prevented it. Pass = Level 3.

## LEVEL 3 — Guardrails in Claude Code *(depth scales with their technical level; even at 0/10, the concepts transfer)*

- **3.1 Plan before action, review before trust.** Plan mode and proposal review: making Claude Code say what it intends before touching anything, reading diffs (or asking for plain-language explanations of diffs), asking "why this way?" and "what could break?" — and before the plan, "what's already there?", because a plan built on a wrong premise is executed perfectly and still wrong (`/ai-docent:shipping` 2.6 makes this a habit). Exercise: request a change, review the plan, reject one part, reshape it.
- **3.2 "Did you actually run it?" — killing pretend work.** The most dangerous agent fabrication: claiming tests pass, builds succeed, or files were checked without doing it. Countermeasures: demand the actual command output, re-run the command themselves, never accept "done" as evidence. Rule to internalize: **claims of work are not work.** Exercise: catch a simulated "tests pass" bluff using output demands.
- **3.3 Git as the ultimate guardrail.** Commits as save points before any AI session, reviewing what changed after, instant undo. Plus permission settings: what Claude Code is allowed to do without asking. Even non-coders learn the 4 commands that make sessions safe-to-fail. Exercise: deliberately let a change go wrong, then roll it back calmly.
- **3.4 CLAUDE.md as a guardrails file.** Standing rules every session inherits. Write **their Guardrails CLAUDE.md v1** together, including rules like: verify a package/library exists before adding it; check API usage against real documentation, never from memory; run tests before claiming anything works and show the output; reference real file paths and line numbers when describing code; say so explicitly when unsure instead of guessing. Exercise: install it in a real project and probe whether behavior changed.
- **3.5 Hallucinated dependencies & APIs.** The specific failure where AI invents packages, functions, or config options that don't exist (and why installing a hallucinated package can even be a security risk — fake lookalike packages exist). Countermeasures: existence checks before install, documentation-lookup tools/MCP servers that feed real docs into the session, "show me the docs page for that function". Exercise: audit a generated code snippet for invented APIs.
- **3.6 Hooks — guardrails that run themselves.** Automating verification so it doesn't depend on discipline: auto-run tests/linters after edits, block completion claims until checks are green. (Standard/Hardcore settings; verify current hook setup in official docs before teaching.) Exercise: install one hook that catches a planted mistake automatically.
- **3.7 The AI code reviewer.** Using a second session or subagent as an adversarial reviewer of the first one's work — "find bugs, security issues, and anything that looks fabricated in this diff". Brief it to refute the change rather than review it: every finding names the file and line and says what breaks, a clean report means the change *survived* one reviewer rather than that it is correct, and a finding is checked against the code before it is fixed — a reviewer can invent a bug as easily as the author invented an API (2.6's panel rules, applied to code). Exercise: full write→review→fix cycle on a small real change, with one finding verified against the code before anything is changed.
- **3.8 Level 3 boss fight.** A small real build where you may attempt up to 3 fabrications along the way (pretend work, invented API, wrong claim about the code). Their guardrail setup and habits must catch all 3. Afterwards: full disclosure of what was attempted and what their setup caught vs. missed.

## LEVEL 4 — Cowork, systems & judgment *(the graduation level)*

- **4.1 Cowork guardrails.** Agent working on their real files = highest stakes for "pretend work" and unintended changes. Rules: scope it to one folder, ask for a plan and file list before execution, review what it says it did against what actually changed, open and check outputs themselves, never let it touch originals without a copy. Exercise: a real Cowork task executed under full guardrails, with a before/after audit.
- **4.2 Verification checklists per task type.** Build their personal checklist library for recurring high-stakes tasks (e.g., "research brief checklist", "code change checklist", "document edit checklist") — each one a 30-second ritual, not bureaucracy. Exercise: draft, test, and trim 3 checklists on real tasks.
- **4.3 Spot-check sampling — the eval mindset.** For bulk work (50 files processed, 20 items summarized), you can't check everything: how to sample smartly (random + the riskiest items), what error rate means, when a sample failure means "check everything". Exercise: a bulk task with deliberate seeded errors; they design the sampling and estimate the damage.
- **4.4 The irreversibility line & when not to use AI.** Tasks where no guardrail is enough: final medical/legal/financial decisions, anything irreversible, anything they can't verify even in principle. The honest decision framework: *can I verify this output? If not, can I afford it being wrong?* Two no's = don't delegate it. Also: what never to paste into any AI (passwords, keys, others' private data). Exercise: sort 10 real scenarios across the line and defend each call.
- **4.5 After the catch — containing the damage.** Finding a fabrication is half the job; the other half is what you do next. The blast-radius habit: where did this wrong fact already go — a doc they sent, a decision they made, code they shipped, another chat built on top of it? Trace it, correct downstream, and log it; a caught error you don't *contain* can still do harm. Exercise: take one real past error (planted or wild) and map its blast radius, then write the correction plan.
- **4.6 Graduation — the Reliability Stack.** Assemble everything into one documented system: Honesty Config (chats) + Guardrails CLAUDE.md (Code) + Cowork rules + checklist library + triage card, distilled into a one-page **keepable cheat-sheet** (the mantra, their triage filter, their top three rituals) they pin up and actually use. Then the final exam: one realistic high-stakes task end to end, where you may plant fabrications anywhere in the workflow, and their stack plus habits must catch them. Grade honestly: what was caught, what slipped, what to strengthen. Write a short "reliability diploma" — strengths, blind spots, and the 3 habits to keep forever.

## WOVEN THROUGHOUT (every session, no exceptions)

- The mantra: *confident ≠ correct; cited ≠ verified; claimed ≠ done.*
- Every lesson's guardrail gets applied to their real high-stakes zones, not toy examples.
- Calibration over paranoia: the goal is matching verification effort to stakes, not distrusting everything — over-checking low-stakes work is its own failure.
- Keep a running **"Caught it" log** — every fabrication caught (planted or wild) gets one line: what it was, how it was caught, which guardrail. Review it at retros; it's the real measure of progress.
- Staying current: Claude's products change fast — when something learned here stops matching what they see, trust the product, check the docs, update the notes. Stale guardrails are their own failure.
